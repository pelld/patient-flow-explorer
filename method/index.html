How the scores work

An adapted GRADE approach for health-services evidence.

What the evidence score means

The score shows how confident we are that the reported relationship is reliable enough to inform a decision. It does not say whether an intervention is a good idea.

GRADE was developed mainly for clinical intervention evidence. This site adapts its logic for routine data, natural experiments and other health-services research.

1. Start from the strongest study design

4/4

High

Randomised evidence or several strong causal evaluations giving consistent results.

3/4

Moderate

Controlled quasi-experimental evidence, such as a strong difference-in-differences or interrupted time series.

2/4

Low

Adjusted observational, panel, ecological or analytical cross-sectional evidence.

1/4

Very low

Uncontrolled before-and-after studies, small surveys, local descriptive studies or expert opinion.

2. Check six domains

Risk of bias

Could confounding, reverse causation, selection or recall bias explain the result?

Consistency

Do independent studies using different data and methods agree?

Precision

Is the estimate precise enough to distinguish a useful effect from little or no effect?

Directness

Does the evidence measure the actual NHS population, intervention and outcome?

Publication bias

Could published research over-represent positive or unusual results?

Other factors

A large effect, dose-response pattern or agreement across independent methods may raise confidence.

3. Reach the final score

A serious concern normally lowers the score by one level. A very serious concern may lower it by two. Convincing additional evidence may raise it by one.

A systematic review does not automatically score highly. The score depends on the quality and consistency of the studies inside it.

Association stays separate

The association label describes the observed relationship: strong, moderate, weak, none or unknown. The evidence score describes how much confidence to place in that label.
